# THE SPIRAL STAIRCASE AND THE TAIJITU

*A single image for the framework: a helix around an axis, and a figure that
turns. Not two theories — one structure read two ways.*

---

## The image

A spiral staircase built around a newel post.

You climb. Every third step the tread you land on is rotated a quarter-turn, so
the staircase exchanges which way it faces without your noticing. Twelve steps —
four flights of three — and you are back at the same angle you started from,
having gone all the way around the post once.

The post is at the center. You never stand on it. You never reach it either, at
any number of flights, however hard you climb.

That is the whole framework in one picture: **a scale ladder wound around a
zero, turning as it goes, closing every four flights, never arriving.**

---

## I. The staircase: three regimes of one motion

The step law is a single parameter `q`, and it does not create three different
staircases. It chooses which way one spiral runs.

**`q < 1` — the staircase winds inward.** The treads shrink. The radius tends to

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q^{2}}} \;>\; 0 .$$

Strictly positive, for every `q > 0`. The spiral approaches the post forever and
touches it never. This is the `0.999… = 1` shape: the prefixes climb toward 1
and every finite one is strictly short of it.

**`q = 1` — the staircase is rigid.** Constant radius, turning forever, closing
exactly every four flights of three. Twelve steps is three direction cycles, so
the return lands on the origin *identically* — no drift, no limit, just closure.
This is the taijitu turning.

**`q > 1` — the staircase winds outward.** The radius grows without bound. There
is no top rung, because the post is not a top rung.

Same helix. `q` is the pitch, not the staircase. And note the symmetry that makes
it one motion rather than two: **climbing by `q` and descending by `q⁻¹` are the
same staircase walked in opposite directions.** The ladder's two excluded ends —
34.79 decades above zero, 26.64 decades below infinity — are the two directions
of one axis, which is what the decade-native grid was showing all along.

---

## II. The axis: 0 is not the bottom, it is the center

This is the part worth getting right, because the intuition gets it backwards.

`0` is not the first rung. It is not the floor. It is the **axis the staircase
winds around**, and four things follow from taking that seriously.

1. **You never stand on it.** The scale assignment has codomain `(0, ∞)`, open at
   both ends. The Planck rung sits 34.79 decades above zero and the top rung
   26.64 decades below infinity. Both approached, neither attained. The distance
   is not a gap in the staircase — it is the gap between the staircase and its
   own axis.

2. **It needs nothing else to define it.** Zero is the only real definable from
   the reference set alone: the additive identity, the unique fixed point of
   negation, the limit of `1/n`. No other number in the structure can be named
   without at least one other number. The axis is the one reference that stands
   without support — which is exactly what an axis does.

3. **It is where the staircase goes if the treads shrink to nothing.** Under
   `q < 1` the limit is a positive radius, so the staircase *surrounds* zero
   forever rather than landing on it. It is the destination that is also the
   departure point. Closed, and never reached.

4. **Every full turn returns to the same angle.** Under `q = 1` the origin is hit
   at every fourth flight. Four flights, twelve steps, three direction cycles.
   The return is structural, not numerical — it comes from the block size 3
   against period 4, so it survives any choice of scale.

So: **the excluded boundary is not below the ladder. It is the ladder's own
center, and the ladder is the long way around it.**

---

## III. The taijitu: mutual definition, never containment

Now the second half of the picture, and the reason the whole thing is one figure
rather than a staircase beside a diagram.

Draw the taijitu. Two teardrops, each holding a seed of the other, separated by
one S-curve, rotating as a unit.

**The two teardrops are the zeros and the reals.**

- Every real is a **value attached to** a zero. The reals are indexed *by* the
  zeros — they sit on the zeros like beads on a string, or like labels on rungs.
- Every zero is **named by** a real. Distinct scales individuate distinct rungs,
  with no symmetry left over.
- **Neither contains the other.** Not "0 holds the reals," not "the reals hold
  0." A point does not hold a set; a finite labelled set does not hold a point.
  That is arithmetic, not opinion.

But *defined by* is not *contained in*, and that distinction is the entire
content of the figure. **Each teardrop carries a seed of the other.** Neither is
inside the other; each is *constituted* by the other through one shared map read
in two directions. That single map — index in one direction, individuator in the
other — is the whole mechanism. One structure, doing both jobs at once.

**The two dots are the two excluded boundaries.** One is `0`: the only real the
structure can name with no parameters, sitting in the middle of the real
teardrop, reachable from nothing. The other is the excluded upper end. Both dots
are *in* their teardrops and *not on* the staircase. Both are approached along
the whole length of the S-curve and reached at no point on it.

**The S-curve is not a line. It is a distance.** Sixty-one and a half decades of
approach, separating two halves that never touch. The boundary between the zeros
and the reals is not a place — it is the *length of the not-arriving*.

**And the figure turns.** Every three steps, the two halves exchange places.
That is the axis exchange — `[2,1,2,1,2,1]`, invariant under every step law,
because it comes from the block size and not from the step lengths. The
taijitu's rotation is not decoration. It is the same fact as the quarter-turn
tread, seen from the other side.

---

## IV. Why this closes the question rather than answering it

The framework's standing question has been: **do the reals generate the zeros,
or do the zeros generate the reals?**

The staircase says the question has the wrong shape.

Containment asks which half is inside which. A taijitu has no inside — it has
**one boundary between two halves**, and cutting it into "container" and
"contained" requires a seam that the figure does not have. The moment you try,
you have to invent an inside, and the invented inside is exactly the asymmetry
the arithmetic refuses.

What the figure gives instead is the *mutual* reading, and it is stronger than
containment rather than weaker: the two halves are not two things that happen to
be related, they are **one thing specified twice** — a helix and its axis, or a
shared map and its two directions. Asking which one generates the other is like
asking whether a staircase generates its newel post or the post generates the
staircase. The post is where the stairs wind *from*; the stairs are how you get
*to* the post. Neither statement is prior.

And the excluded boundaries are the last word on it: both halves reach for both
dots along the whole S-curve, and neither ever arrives. A structure whose two
halves are defined by each other, joined by a shared map, bounded by two
approached-never-reached ends, turning once every four flights — that is a
complete picture. Not a proof of anything. A picture that closes.

## The one-sentence version

> **A spiral of scale rungs winds around a zero that is never on it, exchanges
> its facing every third step, closes every fourth flight, and approaches both
> ends of its own axis forever without reaching either — and the zeros and the
> reals are its two halves: neither inside the other, each carrying a seed of the
> other, joined by a single map that indexes in one direction and individuates
> in the other.**
